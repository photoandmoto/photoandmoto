It is a Thursday evening somewhere in southern France. A van with Finnish license plates and an 'SF' sticker on the back door crawls along a narrow country road. Its headlights, which are certainly not LEDs, barely distinguish the hedges and telephone poles from the darkness.

In the passenger seat, the mechanic holds a Michelin map that stopped being useful about twenty kilometers ago. Somewhere around here, there should be a motocross track. Somewhere, there should be a hand-painted sign nailed to a tree.

No phone to call the organizer. No navigator screen showing a blue destination point. Only patience, instinct, and the hope that someone in the next village can point the way.

This was a daily routine for drivers who carved their way to racing careers across Europe at a time when 'EU' was just the French verb for 'to be' and the internet was, at best, science fiction. Back then, going abroad to compete wasn't just a trip; it was an expedition.

### Lost translations and broken lines

The season had to be built on paper long before reaching the first paddock gate in France. Regarding the competitions, it was the manager's—or promoter's—job. They knew the organizers, booked the races, and negotiated the money. That money consisted of two parts: the starting fee for getting to the line and the prize money based on the placement.

And a name had its price. Once a driver had a few races behind them and proof of results, the starting fees rose and doors opened to larger events with bigger rewards. Until then, a driver from the distant north was just a name without a face, and their value was accordingly low.

English was not yet widely or commonly spoken. The role of Google Translate was filled by a French-English dictionary printed in small font. Translating was about as fast as a turtle in a hundred-meter dash. The promoter could write English at a very basic level at best, but spoke it hardly at all. In the French countryside, few spoke anything other than French. Letters and telexes traveled back and forth in stiff English, and both parties did their best to guess between the lines.

![Correspondence between the driver and the manager](/images/PXL_20261029_172924627~2.jpg "Correspondence between the driver and the manager")

Everything moved at the speed of mail. Contracts typed on a typewriter using the one-finger technique traveled north in 'par avion' stamped envelopes; they were signed, sealed, and sent back. The round trip took weeks.

When the season was underway, the driver lived in the van on the road. Thus, they became difficult to reach. So, the promoter might send letters to a 'poste restante' address at a post office near the next race, and the driver would pick them up at the counter by showing their passport.

International calls were expensive, and since there was no common spoken language, a call could cost a fortune without clarifying anything. The fastest channel was the telex, which represented the instant messaging of the time, provided you were standing in the local post office at the exact moment of transmission. Telegrams were saved for emergencies.

### Competing on a small budget forced tightening the belt

In Finland's small motorsport market, sponsors were a rare treat, and there were plenty of hungry mouths around the table. If you were lucky, a few small local companies would join the effort. If you were really lucky, the bike importer would come to the rescue.

Racing gear was one thing that was in good shape in Finland. At that time, the country was the promised land of motocross equipment, the home base of Sinisalo and Yoko. But no one went bankrupt buying gloves. Bikes, spare parts, fuel, ferry tickets, and months on the road burned through funds, and most of it was paid out of the driver's own pocket.

Even the bank was not a real option. You could put on your best shirt and go in hat in hand, only to hear the familiar phrase: 'No thank you, they said at the bank.'

Every race had to produce results, or an premature trip back to Finland via Värtan would begin ahead of schedule. The money from one weekend paid for a week of living and the drive to the next location. However, the will to be at the top one day was relentlessly strong.

### Getting there involved detours

Viewed from Finland, the rest of Europe felt significantly further away than it does today. Even though the globe was exactly the same size, the whole world seemed much larger back then.

First was the ferry trip to Sweden or directly to Germany, and then hour after hour on motorways, or rather, toll-free side roads. One van carried everything: bikes, spare parts, tools, a tent, food, and people. The team consisted of the driver, the mechanic, and perhaps a family member who also served as the cook, timekeeper, and emotional support.

![The journey toward the open sea and southern Europe often started here](/images/Screenshot%202026-10-30%20113348.jpg "The journey toward the open sea and southern Europe often started here")

In the Nordic countries, passports could stay in the glove compartment, but further south, every border was an event in itself. Engine off, passports out, and a customs officer peering into the packed van, wondering who the motorcycles and other items were actually for. The paperwork, the 'ATA Carnet', had to prove that the bike and spare parts would also leave the country and would not be sold there. One missing stamp, and the afternoon was lost.

However, this was only the Western European part. If the destination was behind the Iron Curtain, say Czechoslovakia, the process was significantly more complicated. The trip required a visa obtained well in advance, and at the border, armed guards and a thorough inspection of the van, down to every spare part, awaited. Nowadays, you can drive across most European borders without slowing down.

Wallet—or rather, wallets contained different currencies. On the road, you needed at least marks, crowns, D-marks, and francs. Currencies had to be exchanged in advance at banks. If you ran out of cash during the weekend, it was impossible to get help quickly. The card carried along was not plastic, but a card for a girlfriend, and even that was often without a stamp.

### Life on the road

And then there were the tracks. Natural terrain, deep ruts, mud, and sand, which were rarely leveled between heats. After a long drive and a night spent in a van, competing on them required a different kind of resilience that no stopwatch could measure. Sometimes, after a night spent in the paddock following a race, the free combination of Dubonnet and Ricard Anisette offered in the evening might throb in your head, whether you were celebrating a well-run race or mourning an unfortunate failure.

![A time when motocross tracks were still naturally created](/images/PICT004494.JPG "A time when motocross tracks were still naturally created")

The van was simultaneously a workshop, a spare parts warehouse, a kitchen, and a bedroom, regardless of the time of day. Evenings were spent in an open field under a canopy, repairing engines, straightening handlebars, and fixing everything that the weekend had broken.

Next-day deliveries were unknown. If something tore, it was welded, a local machine shop was found, or parts were borrowed from a neighboring car. The paddock was a community facing the same challenges. Competitors on Sunday, neighbors for the rest of the week, because everyone knew that their turn would come eventually.

![Fresh air and the French countryside](/images/PICT004493.JPG "Fresh air and the French countryside")

Contacting home required locating a public phone, feeding coins or local tokens into the device, and getting the message across quickly. Often, there was no contact with the driver for days, and information traveled with a delay, if at all.

Nowadays, all of this is handled with a smartphone in your pocket.

### Reality lives behind the pictures

It is easy to identify with the drivers in the pictures, whether on the starting line, in the air, or covered in mud. Yet, everything that happened before the 'H-hour' often remains invisible. The letters, telexes, long ferry trips, border control queues, and evenings spent in a field repairing the bike all took their toll. The time spent in the actual competition was only a fraction of the work and effort that arriving at the race paddock actually required.
