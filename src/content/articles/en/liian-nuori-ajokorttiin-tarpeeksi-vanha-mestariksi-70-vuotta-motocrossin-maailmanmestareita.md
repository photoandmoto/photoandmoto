Imagine this: It is the year 2011, a long MX2 season has come to an end, and a young German rider steps onto the top step of the podium as a world champion. Ken Roczen is 17 years old. In most of Europe, he is too young to get a driver's license, so he can't even legally drive himself home from the track. Yet, he has just defeated the planet's best MX2 riders in a season-long battle.

Let's fast-forward forty years. In the early 1970s, world champions were typically closer to thirty, and not a single teenager had ever won a world title. The first one wouldn't be seen until 1980.

This raises a question that we just couldn't get out of our heads: when is a motocross rider actually in their prime? We found out by going through every men's world champion since 1957 (79 riders, 174 championships). The answer turned out to be a much more interesting story than expected.

### When champions were mature men

In the 1960s and 70s, suspension was so primitive that the riders themselves acted as part of the shock absorbers. There were also significantly fewer titles up for grabs: the 500cc World Championship started in 1957, the 250cc in 1962, and the 125cc class didn't arrive until 1975. For most of that era, there simply wasn't a dedicated class for young riders to make their breakthrough.

The champions of that era prove this to be true. Belgium's Roger De Coster, whom everyone nicknamed "The Man," won his 500cc championships between the ages of 26 and 31. Finland's Heikki Mikkola grabbed his first world championship at 29 and his last in 1978 at the age of 33. Neither of them was any teenage prodigy.

![Heikki Mikkola thanking for the victory at the Ruskeasanta 500cc GP](/images/mIKKOLA.jpg "Heikki Mikkola thanking for the victory at the Ruskeasanta 500cc GP")

> There was, of course, an exception: Joël Robert won his first 250cc crown at age 20 and followed it up with five more. However, the vast majority of champions from that era didn't take their titles until they were past the age of 25.

### The 1980s was the rise of the youth

Then something changed. In 1980, 19-year-old Belgian Georges Jobé won the 250cc world championship as the sport's first-ever champion under twenty. Eric Geboers did the same feat, also at 19 years old. Then in 1991, 18-year-old Stefan Everts grabbed the 125cc title – the first of the ten world championships he would eventually accumulate.

![Georges Jobe at the 1981 Russian 250 GP](/images/jobe.jpg "Georges Jobe at the 1981 Russian 250 GP")

> What changed? The most obvious factor was the 125cc class introduced in 1975, which offered young riders their own path to becoming a world champion. There are other possible reasons in the background, which we will return to at the end.

### The era of young stars

Fast-forward to today: teenage champions have become considerably more common. Remember Roczen, the 17-year-old mentioned at the beginning? He has plenty of company: Jeffrey Herlings at 17, Jorge Prado at 17. Antonio Cairoli won his first title at 19 and eventually racked up a staggering nine championships. Tim Gajser also took his at 19.

![Antonio Cairoli won his first title at 19, eventually reaching nine championships](/images/Cairoli.jpg "Antonio Cairoli won his first title at 19, eventually reaching nine championships")

Many of today's champions have progressed through systematic junior series and factory team development programs. They reach the World Championship level young, but they already have years of racing experience behind them.

Rules play a part too. The MX2 class has an upper age limit that forces riders to move on in their early twenties. So if you want to take the MX2 title, you have to do it young – by the rules of the sport, every MX2 champion is young by definition.

Across all champions, the average age for the first world title has dropped from around 25 in the 1960s and 70s to around 21 today. That's a drop of about four years in a sport where a top career might last only a decade or so.

### So at what age are champions at their best?

Here comes the surprising twist. Teenage winners grab the newspaper headlines, but they don't win the majority of championships. When all world championships ever won are ordered by age, the most productive age window is still 23 to 28 years old. About half of all championships have been won within that bracket.

![Top riders start winning younger.](/images/title_age_shift_en.png "Top riders start winning younger.")

Instead, the change is visible in where the peak phase is located within this age window. The champions of past decades were sharpest at 26–28 years old. After 1990, the busiest phase has shifted slightly earlier, to 23–25 years of age. So riders are reaching their peak younger than before, but it's a minor shift, not a complete revolution.

> Our guess as to why: a young rider can be really fast, but a championship is decided over the course of a whole season, and building consistency usually takes a few extra years. Pure speed gets you a race win, but a championship requires much more.

### Those who broke the mold

Then there are the riders who don't care about these patterns at all – and that's exactly why we love this sport.

Yves Demaria won his first world championship in the MX3 class at age 32 and his last at 35. Out of the 174 championships in history, only nine have been won at age 32 or older, and Demaria accounts for a full third of them.

![Yves Demaria bounced back from this crash at the 1990 Italian 125cc GP to become the MX3 class champion at age 32.](/images/yves%20de%20maria.jpg "Yves Demaria bounced back from this crash at the 1990 Italian 125cc GP to become the MX3 class champion at age 32.")

Stefan Everts took his first title as a teenager and his tenth at 33, fifteen years later. That means a fifteen-year gap between his first and last world gold. When he won his first, some of today's champions weren't even born.

Romain Febvre rose to MXGP champion in 2015 and then had to wait ten years for his second crown, which came in 2025. Ten years between championships, and he took his second title at age 33.

These examples are a fitting reminder that an athlete's peak age is a statistical trend, not a law of nature. The average only reveals what usually happens; it cannot tell you what an unrelenting and exceptionally talented athlete is capable of.

### A question with many answers

So top riders start winning younger. That part is clear. The bigger mystery is whether they are also able to stay winners longer.

On paper, today's stars have longer championship runs than the heroes of the 1970s, but that is largely explained by starting early. Whether they can still win at ages 33 or 35 is something we simply cannot know yet, as the riders providing the answer are only now reaching that age. Herlings has won championships from age 17 all the way into his thirties. Gajser and Prado likewise have years ahead of them.

What is behind this development, then? Statistics show a trend, not the underlying cause. But if this were being discussed over a pint, our best guesses would be the following:

- **A dedicated pathway has been created for youth.** First the 125cc and nowadays MX2 with its age limits offer young riders a genuine opportunity for a world title. That is likely the single biggest reason why the first championships are achieved earlier than before.
- **Riding starts younger.** Youth series and factory team academy programs guide riders into a professional career even before school ends.
- **The sport has become a full-time job.** Factory teams handle physical training, equipment, travel, and medical services year-round. In the early decades, such background support was much rarer.
- **Medicine has taken leaps forward.** Advanced surgical care and rehabilitation may partly explain why riders are able to return to the top even after long breaks. Herlings' new championship in 2026 represents precisely the kind of comeback this could explain. This cannot, however, be conclusively proven without injury statistics.
- **Equipment and money.** Modern bikes are different, though not obviously easy to master. Larger contract money, on the other hand, might either motivate continuing a career longer or, conversely, enable retiring earlier. In reality, no one knows for sure which way the scales tip.

These are the theories we would put on the table ourselves. You might have your own.

So the next time you see a teen win a world championship round, enjoy it. Just don't bet against the rider competing around age 25 when the entire championship is decided.
