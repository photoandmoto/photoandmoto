Small and densely populated, Belgium, located in Western Europe, transformed from the 1960s onwards into the main stage and a place of pilgrimage for international motocross, where riders, mechanics, engineers, and team managers gathered.

Success relied on an ideal logistical location, deep national passion, the economic volume of the sport, technological innovations, and excellent training conditions.

This article discusses Belgium's role as a global hub for the sport, its significance to Finnish and other riders, Belgian innovations, and the factors that eventually led to the decline of the era.

### **Beringen and its surroundings initially served as the base for the Finns**

The international breakthrough and rise of Finnish motocross to the world summit is inextricably linked to Belgium. As the sport began to professionalize, Finnish pioneers like Heikki Mikkola quickly understood that lasting success required more than just occasional, exhausting travel from Finland to Central European GP races. In the early 1970s, Mikkola made a strategic decision and established his base in Belgium, later in the small town of Beringen, located in the Flanders region. This move created a standard that many of Master Mikkola's followers, aiming for the top, quickly adopted as their own.

In the early stages, when resources were still limited and operations were finding their form, several Finnish riders stayed at a small hotel in the center of [Beringen](https://maps.app.goo.gl/x9qEnqYBG2kKspDP7) called [Brasserie Thier](https://www.facebook.com/p/Brasserie-Thier-100094879781948/). The owner family of this hotel became very familiar to the Finns and formed an important social anchor in a foreign country.

![Hotel Brasserie Thier in the center of Beringen](/images/beringen.jpg "Hotel Brasserie Thier in the center of Beringen")

Another popular option was the campsite located in the immediate vicinity of the Zolder race track, which served as a cost-effective solution and allowed for close community with other competitors.

![In the picture, Finns at the Zolder campsite in 1979. With success and growing professionalism, most riders moved to rent their own houses in the area. Living in houses was naturally more home-like and provided a more stable environment, better supporting family life for the long and grueling training and racing seasons.

![A happy group of Finns in the yard of a detached house. Jukka Sintonen, Matti Tarkkonen, and Tapani Pikkarainen with their support teams](/images/Screenshot%202026-09-21%20153405.jpg "A happy group of Finns in the yard of a detached house. Jukka Sintonen, Matti Tarkkonen, and Tapani Pikkarainen with their support teams")

Local background figures who welcomed the newcomers from the north with open arms played a central role in the successful integration of the Finns into Belgian society and the sport's culture.

![Finnish motocross riders and support teams after soccer practice in Belgium in the 1980s.](/images/Screenshot%202026-09-21%20153522.jpg "Finnish motocross riders and support teams after soccer practice in Belgium in the 1980s.")

Belgians were generally known for being very helpful toward the Finns. For example, the couple René and Angela Liekens became particularly significant. The Liekens acted as managers, interpreters, agents, and local guides, helping the Finns navigate Belgian bureaucracy, negotiate with local parties, and maintain vital relationships with race organizers.

### **The Belgian magnet: Location, economic volume, and sports culture**

Why was Belgium such an overwhelmingly popular destination not only for Finns but for top riders from dozens of other countries? The answer lies in macro-level factors that combined geography, climate, and an exceptional sports culture favoring motocross.

### **Logistical superiority and track network**

Located in the heart of Europe, Belgium was an ideal base for riders touring the Motocross World Championship series and other competitions. More direct connections to France, Italy, the Netherlands, Germany, and the UK reduced the strain and costs of travel during a time when equipment was moved by vans and trailers, leaving more time for training and recovery.

![Håkan Carlqvist moved to Yamaha in 1980 and tested his bike in Genk in the spring of the same year. Graham Noyce studied the rear suspension in the same place.](/images/Screenshot%202026-09-21%20154010.jpg "Håkan Carlqvist moved to Yamaha in 1980 and tested his bike in Genk in the spring of the same year. Graham Noyce studied the rear suspension in the same place.")

In addition, from the 1960s until the end of the 1990s, an extremely dense network of motocross tracks was located in the Flanders and Wallonia regions. Dozens of different tracks, from deep sand to hard clay and wooded slopes, were located just short drives from each other. This enabled the most versatile and efficient riding training within short distances. Belgium's temperate climate allowed for a significantly longer riding training season than the harsher conditions of Northern Europe.

### **The absolute kings of the tracks**

The Citadelle in Namur, located in Wallonia, was its aristocratic and majestic gem. The race held in Namur between 1947 and 2007 was regularly called the "Monaco of motocross." The track was not located in a traditional motorsport area, but was temporarily cleared in the rugged park, forests, and walkways of the historic fortress (Citadelle) rising above the city of Namur. ### **National passion and economic volume**

The foundation of the Belgian ecosystem was the exceptional national popularity the sport enjoyed. For a long time, motocross held almost the status of a national sport in the country, and it was strongly supported at all levels. National and international competitions drew full grandstands of fans, and the audience numbers were huge.

![Jukka Silvennoinen at the Genk international competition in 1981](/images/Screenshot%202026-09-21%20153642.jpg "Jukka Silvennoinen at the Genk international competition in 1981")

This massive popularity created a strong economic foundation. Corporate sponsorship, ticket revenue, and prize money enabled the professionalization of riders. In Belgium, one competed almost every weekend at the top level against world champions and factory riders. For foreign riders, this offered the best development environment. To develop to the top level, one had to compete weekly against the world's elite. The best tactic adopted by Mikkola and other Finns to beat the Belgians was to learn their secrets on their own tracks and in the same demanding conditions.

### **Center of innovation**

In addition to cultivating exceptional athletes, the Belgian motocross ecosystem served as the industry's most significant incubator for technological innovations.

One of the most revolutionary innovations in the entire motorcycle industry and motocross history, the monoshock suspension, originated in Belgium, and its footprint can still be seen in every modern motorcycle.

Behind this breakthrough was a Liège-based engineer and teacher at a local technical college, Lucien Tilkens. He had already demonstrated his inventive talents earlier by designing, among other things, an early device for the mechanical harvesting of sugar beets. Above all, however, Tilkens was known as a dedicated fan of motocross. He worked for years on the technical committee of the Belgian Motorcycle Federation, and his son Guy Tilkens competed in the sport.

![Yamaha succeeded with monoshock suspension. In the picture, Neil Hudson's bike during Belgian training in 1980.](/images/jammu.jpg "Yamaha succeeded with monoshock suspension. In the picture, Neil Hudson's bike during Belgian training in 1980.")

In addition to monoshock suspension, numerous other technical solutions were developed that improved motorcycle frame structures, suspension, and engines, paving the way for modern bikes.

### **Factory of world champions: Belgium's exceptional dominance**

Belgium's sports culture, dense track network, and immense competitive pressure created an ecosystem that resembled natural selection. Only the strongest, most skilled, and physically most durable riders survived to the top. This mill produced Belgian world champions on an assembly line, making the country the absolute ruler of the sport for several decades.

The numbers describing Belgium's historical dominance are staggering. The country holds the overwhelming record for the number of motocross world championships. 15 different Belgian riders have won a total of 56 FIM world championship titles, and the country leads the GP victory statistics with an inconceivable over 600 race wins. The number is so massive that even the combined wins of France and the Netherlands, in second and third place, do not reach the same.

![Belgian world champions at the 1979 Motocross des Nations: Roger De Coster, Harry Everts, and André Malherbe.](/images/Screenshot%202026-09-21%20153150.jpg "Belgian world champions at the 1979 Motocross des Nations: Roger De Coster, Harry Everts, and André Malherbe.")

### **Why did the Belgian motocross ecosystem decline?**

After a four-decade reign, the Belgian motocross ecosystem began to crumble in the 2000s. The country still produces top talent, but it is no longer the essential "Silicon Valley" of the sport. Nevertheless, a few significant top-level teams still keep their headquarters in Belgium.

![The Kawasaki Racing team, led by Kimi Räikkönen and Antti Pyrhönen, keeps its headquarters near the Lommel track](/images/IceOne%20HQ%20lommel.jpg "The Kawasaki Racing team, led by Kimi Räikkönen and Antti Pyrhönen, keeps its headquarters near the Lommel track")

The main reason for the decline of the sport was Belgian environmental legislation. A law that came into effect in Flanders in 1995 restricted grassroots motocross activity and training with strict noise standards and environmental permits.

Although exception clauses allowed for looser noise restrictions for permanent motorsport areas, the “Not in my back yard” phenomenon became a bigger problem. In densely populated Flanders, housing spread near the tracks, and local residents began to systematically challenge the tracks' environmental permits in court, citing noise nuisance.

On the other hand, the promoter of the World Championship series began to emphasize in its guidelines and strategy more and more maximum commercialization, TV visibility, VIP services, and a completely different level of ancillary facilities. This commercial development inevitably headed on a collision course with traditional tracks.

For example, Namur's historic, long, and challenging track proved problematic in terms of both logistics and other requirements. A similar fate awaited many other iconic and historically significant tracks that no longer bent to the requirements of the new business model, not just in Belgium, but throughout the world.
